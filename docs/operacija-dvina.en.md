## Remote episodes (listen before the trip)

### 1. The Forest That Wasn't on the Maps

Picture this: you're walking through the Plokštinė forest and right under your feet — a secret base. [S1] Why exactly here? After the Americans started building underground military bases, the Soviet Union needed to keep its military priority. [S1] The spot by Lake Plateliai was suitable because of its almost 170 meters of height above sea level, light sandy soils and small population. [S1] Here in September 1960, they started building the first underground missile base in the Soviet Union. [S1] The base was built by 10,000 soldier-builders. [S1] With shovels, like wells, they dug 27-meter-deep missile launch silos. [S1] The complex consisted of an underground technological block with 4 underground missile launch silos dug around it. [S1] In the centre they dug a pit, concreted it, installed the equipment, put on a roof and covered it with liquid glass to keep water out. [S1] Then they hid everything under a layer of earth. [S1] The work was finished on December 31, 1962, and that evening, at 22:00, the first combat duty began. [S1] But what's waiting for you at one of those silos — you'll only find out when you come to Plokštinė?

### 2. Target: Europe

Picture this: four R-12 thermonuclear missiles are being lowered into the underground silos. [S1] The Americans called them SS-4s. [S1] One missile with its warhead was 23 metres tall, with a diameter of 1.65 metres. [S1] Its yield was from 1 to 2.3 megatons. [S1] These were medium-range missiles that could fly up to 2000 kilometres. [S1] From Plokštinė, you could aim a missile at any country in Europe. [S1] While the base was active, the missiles were aimed at Norway, Great Britain, Spain, West Germany and Turkey. [S1] Every 3–4 years, the targeting direction was changed. [S1] And now — Cuba: soldiers from the 79th Missile Regiment took part in the events in Cuba. [S1] In September 1962, missiles were transported out of Šateikiai. [S1] They were loaded at night, the soldiers worked dressed in civilian clothes, and everything was classified. [S1] Later, the missiles were taken to Sevastopol, loaded onto cargo ships and shipped to Havana. [S1] Soldiers from the 79th Missile Regiment were setting up the missiles in central Cuba until January 1963. [S1] But what else is waiting for you down in the silo itself — you'll only find out when you come to Plokštinė?

### 3. The Day They Left

Imagine the day the underground watch comes to an end. Throughout the whole time the missile base existed, not a single missile was ever launched. [S1] The troops pulled out of the Plokštinė thermonuclear missile launch complex on June 18, 1978. [S1] The main equipment was dismantled in 1981. [S3] After the Soviet troops left, the abandoned and unmaintained base was badly wrecked by metal thieves. [S3] The barracks buildings in Plokštinė were reconstructed, and for a while a pioneer camp operated here. [S3] But the story didn't end here. In 1992 the missile base was handed over to Žemaitija National Park. [S3] In 1996 the base was minimally adapted for visitors, and the Cold War Museum was officially opened at the end of 2012. [S3] It's the only museum of its kind in Europe and one of just a few in the world. [S3] The exhibition displays a restored fragment of the multi-level electronic security system. [S3] One silo was adapted for visitors, while the other three, heavily damaged, were conserved. [S3] What does the only visitor-ready silo look like today – you'll only see it when you come to Plokštinė?
